Trading bot 0x51C7…2A7F: how it trades, where its edge comes from, what it makes

This contract holds about $73.2M of tokens and trades them all day. We read its verified code and every role grant from its multisig. Then we read all 31,737 transactions that moved its tokens in one day. The Open Labels Initiative tags it “Wintermute_1” and a funder “Wintermute 4”. Tags, not proof (note 7).

For readers who know AMMs, RFQ and block building. About fifteen minutes. Every figure links to its source or comes from read-only RPC calls.

1. How one trade makes money

An operator key swaps against a pool. A solver fills its signed quote. The firm's other contracts swap its tokens. The gain stays in the bot as tokens.

Only 28% of its own swaps gain at the trade. The gain shows later (where the edge comes from).

Signed quotes are rarer. Of 60 sampled outside fills, 7 asked it to vouch. The code: one swap, the quote check.

Third route. The Swap Router, Rizzolver and two “MEV Bot” contracts hold its spender role. We read them as the firm's own (inference). 5,842 of the Swap Router's 7,501 transactions sit beside a bot transaction. They pay gas and tip builders.

2. How the contract works

Four jobs: trade, check quotes, pay out, stop. Each is a function behind a list. A Safe and a registry write the lists. The spender list includes the firm's other contracts (inference, note 8).

Architecture details: the lists and the registry Three contracts, and which one can change what.

A 4-of-10 Safe drives an unverified role registry. The registry writes every list on the bot.

The registry calls the bot's onRoleGranted or onRoleRevoked hooks with a role id and an account. The bot flips a flag in the matching list. Nothing else can change these lists (lines 240–349).

A Uniswap pool joins only if a factory lists it: factory.getPair(token0, token1) == pair. Each grant's data names that factory (note 1). The check is at lines 257–284. Curve pools join with no such check (lines 285–287).

The owner, the registry and the wrapped-ETH contract are fixed at deploy. The bot has no upgrade path. There is no proxy, delegatecall or selfdestruct.

The same registry serves 33 more role ids that this bot never reads. The Safe also calls approveTokens on two sibling bots. So one Safe and one registry run a family of contracts. This page covers one of them.

3. Where the edge comes from

Outside traders filling its signed quotes earn it (inference). Their fills beat Binance that second on 59% of WETH/stable fills. That is +7.0 bp weighted by dollars. On the day: +$70k. Callers pay their own gas.

Its own swaps beat Binance too: +3.3 bp before costs, +$42k. Against Binance, tips and gas of $64k turn that into −$22k. The firm's other contracts stand at +$3k after costs on 4,179 fills.

Which strategy? Four tests

  • Rarely a backrun. 5.1% of its legs trade back another trader's swap. Chance gives 0.1%. So some backrunning happens. At least 79% of its swaps follow a bot or family-contract transaction. That is the bundle signature (inference).
  • Sandwiches are rare. 10 in the day, against 1.8 by chance.
  • Not “buy, wait, sell”. Inventory tokens close in 3 min, as chance does (3 min). Base tokens close in 24 s.
  • Beats Binance. 63% of its 7,540 WETH/stable swaps beat Binance that second. A coin flip gives 50%. The pool then follows it 60% of the time, against 50%.

More evidence: gain over time, sides, sizes and peers

Fact. Its own swaps came to −4.5 bp at the trade. After five minutes they stood at +5.1 bp. After an hour they stood at +5.0 bp.

The five-minute gain is +$88k. That is smaller than the swaps' own tips and gas. Its range stays above zero, at +$25k to +$164k. We resample whole hours, because trades in one hour share one price move.

DefiLlama's 5-minute price lags Binance. That is why own swaps look negative at the trade and positive minutes later (inference).

The trade-time price is interpolated between the two nearest 5-minute points. With the last point before the trade instead, own swaps start at −8.5 bp. The loss gets bigger.

The gain is concentrated in time. Three hours gave 76% of it. Those hours: 02:00 to 05:00 UTC. That is around the day's first big move (inference). Overall, 14 of 24 hours were positive.

What it is, and what it isn't

We read it as the on-chain inventory wallet of a professional market maker (inference). Its keys swap on Uniswap and Curve and tip builders. Solvers and fillers pull its tokens through allowances. Funds arrive from exchange hot wallets and leave to Binance and Circle deposit addresses.

A source comment calls it an “MEV bot and contract wallet” (line 81). Explorer tags say “Market Maker” and “MEV Bot”. Tags are third-party (note 7).

It is not a drainer or a honeypot. No function lets an outsider take its money.

We found peers from 1,307 UniswapX fills by 53 fillers, not from labels. We measured each one the same way over 6.7 hours.

5. How much it made

About +$35k a day over 23 days, after the tips and gas we could count. The 4 Nov fill of $24.1M is part of it. Without it: +$20k. The studied day (15 Nov): +$41k. Range: −⁠$48k to +$128k.

Split per day, against DefiLlama:

  • Quote fills and other outside fills: +$58k a day. 18 of 23 days up. Range +$20k to +$106k. Without the one fill: +$42k.
  • Own swaps: −⁠$28k. 1 of 23 days up. Range −⁠$37k to −⁠$18k.
  • The firm's other contracts: +$5k, after $21k of tips and gas a day.

Verdict. On chain, the bot made about +$35k a day over 23 days. That is measured. The range of the mean is −⁠$3k to +$81k, so 23 days alone do not prove a profit. The firm's three years of running it and its $73.2M balance do, in our reading (inference). Without the one big fill the mean is +$20k, still not a loss.

What we cannot see, and what it does to the sign

  • The exchange side. If the firm hedges on Binance or elsewhere, those trades leave no trace on this chain. The number above is the on-chain leg only (fact).
  • Fees and running costs. Exchange fees, funding, hedge slippage, servers and staff are not in it. Each of these subtracts, so the true profit is likely lower than ours, not higher (inference).
  • An offsetting leg. If these trades are the on-chain half of a pair and the other half loses, part of the gain could be cancelled. We cannot rule that out. The firm keeping it running and funded is the evidence against a net loss (inference).
  • Net. Fact: the on-chain leg is positive. Inference: the whole operation is positive but smaller than +$35k a day.

What these figures can't show

  • A reference price, not cash. Tokens are valued at DefiLlama's price. The bot may hedge or unwind elsewhere.
  • A short window. Days read: 23. The inventory figures are point reads, not a cash ledger.
More limits
  • Reference lag. DefiLlama's 5-minute price may lag Binance. If so, part of the five-minute gain is the reference catching up (inference).
  • The day's price trend. WETH fell that day. The trend-removed figures correct for it. Before that step the total was +$54k. Its range was −$35k to +$152k.
  • Trade-time price. We interpolate it between the two nearest 5-minute points. Using the last point before the trade, own swaps start at −8.5 bp (−$146k). The loss at the trade gets bigger.
  • Only landed trades. Bundles that never landed are not in the gain figures.
  • Price coverage. 93% of trade volume had 5-minute prices on every leg. The other 7% had 15-minute prices and is left out of the curves.
  • Sibling bots. The family's contracts are counted apart from outside fills. The “MEV Bot” siblings alone moved $31.4M.
  • Pool-derived prices. DefiLlama prices most tokens from DEX pools. The at-trade mark can include the trade's own impact. Binance is the independent check.
  • Excluded trades. Trades without 5-minute prices are left out. So are their tips and gas.
  • Tips, an upper bound. A family transaction's tip is charged whole to this bot. A sibling may also trade its own tokens.
  • Gas on other days. Family gas is the studied day's mean per call. Tips are exact.
  • Stable basis. USDC and USDT both count as 1 USDT in the Binance comparison.
  • One hour. With the trend left in, the mean at one hour is +$115k.
  • Flows dwarf the result. That day $123.1M came in and $149.2M went out. Any trading result is small beside them.

The multisig A 4-of-10 Safe that mostly sets allowances.

The owner is a Safe v1.3.0: 4 of 10 owners must sign. It has executed 7,263 transactions, one per nonce. The first was on 8 Aug 2023. 1 more reverted and used no nonce.

Most of its work is allowances: 5,872 calls to the bot's approveTokens. Each one sets ERC-20 allowances from the bot to its spenders. The latest was on 13 Nov 2026.

Role changes go through the registry in two steps: propose, then execute. In the latest pair, a proposal was executed 116 hours later. The median wait across 483 execute calls is 29 hours.

The bot stores an 86,400-second delay but never enforces it. Most registry grants wait longer than that, but not all. 66 came in under 24 hours. 34 of them were a role's first grant. The fastest took 2 minutes. So any delay rule in the registry has exceptions. These counts cover all 46 role ids the Safe touched, not only this bot's. The registry's code is unverified. So the rule behind those exceptions can't be read. 69 proposals (by role and account) were never executed. One call can add several accounts. A re-proposal replaces an older one. So these counts don't subtract.

The owner set changed 15 times by adding and 13 times by removing. The Safe also restarted the bot once, in December 2023. So it had been stopped at least once before.

How it makes money Three engines, then the evidence and its limits.

The bot makes markets with its own inventory. Outside fills of its quotes earn the gain. Its own swaps lose after tips and gas. They gain before (where the edge comes from).

  • Its own swaps. Operators trade against listed AMM pools. They pay builders for a place in the block. That is consistent with CEX–DEX arbitrage or inventory rebalancing.

  • Fills for others. Solvers and fillers use the bot as a quote source. They work on CoW Protocol, UniswapX and aggregators. The firm's other contracts also swap its tokens, and pay for it. They pull its tokens through allowances, or check a signed quote with EIP-1271.

  • Inventory cycling. Tokens arrive from exchange hot wallets and leave to exchange deposit addresses. That day $123.1M came in and $149.2M went out. That fits inventory moving between venues to stay hedged.

The emergency brake What happens when a key pulls the brake.

Any staff key can stop the bot. Only the multisig can start it again.

Pool callbacks don't check the switch. A failed zeroing is only logged. The allowance stays open. The token still drops off the bot's list (lines 416–426).

What a reviewer would flag Design facts a code reviewer would ask about.

Five design facts stand out. None claims that anything has gone wrong.

  1. The registry holds the keys. It names the transferers and the recipients. Together those two lists can send out any token or ETH. The registry's own code is not verified on Sourcify or Blockscout. So its rules, including its delay, can't be read.
  2. The Curve payment trusts the transaction's sender. It pays any listed pool that asks. The pool names the coin and the amount. An operator must have signed the transaction. It checks tx.origin, not whether the bot started the swap. Curve pools join with no factory check (lines 285–287).
  3. A failed revoke drops the token anyway. The token leaves the list first. Then its allowance is zeroed. A log is the only trace of a failure (lines 416–426).
  4. Some results are ignored. Nothing checks that these worked. ETH payouts: line 489. Builder tips: line 686. Pool payments: lines 589, 656, 665, 669, 678.
  5. A stored timelock does nothing here. A one-day delay is set at deploy. It is only read back. This contract never enforces it. See lines 128, 176, 362.

What we checked, and what we didn't How far to trust each figure on this page.

The verified code matches what runs on chain. Equivalence for every input is not proven. The flow figures are one day at 5-minute prices.

Checked

  • Built with its exact compiler settings, the source gives the deployed code byte for byte. Deploy arguments match too.
  • We wrote a shorter, readable copy. It has the same 63 functions, argument types and storage layout.
  • Both ran one test that calls every external function. All 331 compared results matched.
  • We ran both on a local copy of mainnet at block 26,415,084. They gave the same swap output. Both zeroed the same 613 allowances.
  • Role holders: we replayed the Safe's registry calls. They matched the bot's getters for every account.
  • The day: every transaction in the window was fetched with its receipt, none sampled.

Not checked

  • The metadata hash differs. The verified file is a rebuild with stand-in names (note 6).
  • Equivalence for every possible input. Tests are examples, not a proof.
  • The outside contracts: the registry, the multisig's signers, the venues and the spenders.
  • Who runs it. Explorer labels name a firm. We did not confirm them.
  • Prices finer than 5 minutes.
  • 4 of the day's 90 tokens, which had no price. Swaps with an unpriced leg are left out of the gain figures.
  • Any day outside 1 Nov to 14 Nov. The day tables cover one day.

Method

  • Roles. We decoded every Safe execTransaction to the registry. We replayed propose, execute and revoke in order. Then we called the bot's own getter for every replayed holder.
  • Flows. We pulled every ERC-20 Transfer log to or from the bot in the window. Then we fetched each transaction and its receipt. A transaction is a swap if the bot both sent and received tokens. It is funding if it only received, and a payout if it only sent.
  • Markout. For each swap: dollars in minus dollars out, at 5-minute prices. A positive value means the bot gained. In the day tables the range is a bootstrap over transactions.
  • Gain over time. The answers above price each trade again at later minutes. Their ranges resample whole hours, because trades in one hour share one price move. They remove each token's day trend. So a falling market isn't read as skill.
  • Tips and gas. The tip is the first argument of each swap function. Gas is gas used times the effective gas price. Outside callers pay their own gas, so it is not counted. The firm's other contracts pay theirs and tip builders, and both are counted.
  • Inventory. getAllApprovedTokens plus balanceOf and decimals at each snapshot block, priced at that block's time.

Correction. An earlier version left out the firm's other contracts' tips and gas. It showed one day. This one counts them over 23 days, through 2026-11-23.

Words used on this page Short definitions of the terms used above.

Ethereum words

  • MEV. Profit from choosing which trades go in a block, and in what order. ethereum.org explains it.
  • Block builder. The party that packs transactions into a block. The bot reads its address from block.coinbase.
  • WETH. Wrapped ETH: ETH held as a token, so contracts can trade it like one.
  • Allowance. Permission one account gives another to pull a set amount of a token. Defined in ERC-20.
  • Gain. What a trade gained or lost against a reference price. Here that is DefiLlama 5-minute prices, at the trade or minutes later. One basis point (bp) is a hundredth of a percent.

Contract words

  • Multisig. A wallet that needs several keys to sign. This one is a Safe.
  • RFQ. Request for quote: a trader asks a market maker for a signed price.
  • EIP-1271. A standard question a contract answers: did your key sign this?
  • tx.origin. The account that signed the whole transaction, not the direct caller.
  • Solver. A party that settles batches of user orders on CoW Protocol. UniswapX has fillers, which fill single orders. Both can use liquidity such as this bot's.

Trading bot 0x51C7…2A7F: roles, flows and where the money goes

This contract holds about $73.2M of tokens and trades them all day. We read its verified code, then read the chain: every role grant from its multisig, and all 31,737 transactions that moved its tokens in one day.

For readers who know AMMs, RFQ and block building. About fifteen minutes. Every figure links to its source or comes from read-only RPC calls.

Verdict: this is the on-chain inventory wallet of a professional market maker. Its own keys swap on Uniswap and Curve, paying builders for inclusion. Solvers and fillers on CoW Protocol, UniswapX and other routes pull its tokens through allowances. Funds move in from exchange hot wallets and out to deposit addresses labelled Binance and Circle. At 5-minute reference prices the on-chain legs lose money once tips and gas are paid. We infer the profit sits where we can't see it: hedges off this chain, or prices finer than 5 minutes. It is not a drainer and not a honeypot. No function lets an outsider take its money or pulls tokens from anyone else.

A comment in its rebuilt, verified source calls it an “MEV bot and contract wallet” (line 81). Public explorer labels tag it “Market Maker”, “MEV Bot” and, through the Open Labels Initiative, “Wintermute_1”. Labels are third-party tags. We did not verify who runs it (note 7).

1. Architecture: Safe, registry, bot

A 4-of-10 Safe drives an unverified role registry, and the registry writes every list on the bot.

The registry calls the bot's onRoleGranted or onRoleRevoked hooks with a role id and an account. The bot flips a flag in the matching list. Nothing else can change these lists (lines 240–349).

A Uniswap pool joins only if a factory lists it: factory.getPair(token0, token1) == pair. The registry names that factory in the grant's data each time (note 1, lines 257–284). Curve pools join with no such check (lines 285–287).

The owner, the registry and the wrapped-ETH contract are fixed at deploy. The bot has no upgrade path. There is no proxy, delegatecall or selfdestruct.

The same registry serves 33 more role ids that this bot never reads. The Safe also calls approveTokens on two sibling bots. So one Safe and one registry run a family of contracts. This page covers one of them.

3. The multisig

The owner is a Safe v1.3.0: 4 of 10 owners must sign. It has executed 7,264 transactions since 8 Aug 2023.

Most of its work is allowances: 5,873 calls to the bot's approveTokens. Each one sets ERC-20 allowances from the bot to its spenders. The latest was on 13 Nov 2026.

Role changes go through the registry in two steps: propose, then execute. In the latest pair, a proposal was executed 116 hours later. The median wait across 483 execute calls is 29 hours.

The bot stores an 86,400-second delay but never enforces it. Most registry grants wait longer than that, but not all: 66 came in under 24 hours, 34 of them a role's first grant, and the fastest took 2 minutes. So any delay rule in the registry has exceptions. These counts cover all 46 role ids the Safe touched, not only this bot's. The registry's code is unverified, so the rule behind those exceptions can't be read. 69 proposals (by role and account) were never executed. One call can add several accounts, and a re-proposal replaces an older one, so these counts don't subtract.

The owner set changed 15 times by adding and 13 times by removing. The Safe also restarted the bot once, in December 2023, so it had been stopped at least once before.

5. How it makes money

The bot makes markets with its own inventory. Its on-chain trades break even or lose at 5-minute prices. So the edge most likely comes from trades we can't see, or from timing finer than our prices.

  • Its own swaps. Operators trade against listed AMM pools and pay builders for a place in the block. That is consistent with CEX–DEX arbitrage or inventory rebalancing.

  • Fills for others. Solvers and fillers on CoW Protocol, UniswapX and aggregators use the bot as a quote source. They pull its tokens through allowances, or check a signed quote with EIP-1271.

  • Inventory cycling. Tokens arrive from exchange hot wallets and leave to exchange deposit addresses. That day $123.1M came in and $149.2M went out. That fits inventory moving between venues to stay hedged.

We priced every leg at DefiLlama 5-minute prices. Operator swaps came out at −4.9 bp, or −$92k on $188.2M. The 95% range is −$137k to −$36k. That range covers sampling noise across transactions, not error in the reference price. Pool fees alone read as a loss against a mid price. Tips and gas add $117k, or 6.2 bp. All in, the swaps came to −$208k that day (−11.1 bp).

Fills for others came out at −0.3 bp on $344.4M, a range of −$83k to +$76k. That is flat. A maker quoting near the mid looks like this.

A firm that pays $117k a day for inclusion expects to earn more than that. If they act rationally, the edge is in the other leg: a hedge on a centralized exchange, at a better price than our 5-minute reference. That leg is not on this chain. So the chain shows the costs and the volume, not the profit.

6. A profit and loss snapshot

Inventory grew from $8.1M in Sep 2023 to $73.2M now. The chain can't split that growth into deposits and profit.

We read the bot's balance of every approved token, plus its ETH, at 15 blocks, roughly quarterly. We priced each at the block's time. The peak was $74.8M in Mar 2026. On 15 Nov 2026 it held 155 of its 161 approved tokens, with 270.8 ETH. The top 5 tokens make up 64% of the token value. 6 tokens had no price and count as zero.

7. How one swap works

A swap runs only for an operator, on a listed pool, with the bot on. Here is the median-sized v3Swap from our day.

Curve and Uniswap V2 swaps follow the same gates (note 2). Many swap functions have a version that takes a deadline (…DL).

A tip goes only to a listed block builder. It is paid to block.coinbase, so the swap must land in a block that builder built. The bot does not check whether the tip arrived (note 3).

8. How a price quote is checked

The bot acts as an RFQ maker: an off-chain key signs its price quotes, and the bot answers for that key.

The question in the second path is a standard one, called EIP-1271. Only callers on the list may ask it. Revoking the signer also withdraws it from both venues (lines 333–337).

Tokens then leave through allowances. The exits section covers those.

function isValidSignature(bytes32 hash, bytes memory signature) external view whenActive returns (bytes4) {
    require(_approvedRfqCallers_1CiVF7[msg.sender], "H19");

9. Where the money can go

Money leaves the bot by five exits. Each one sits behind a list.

On 14 Nov 2026 the bot was on. It had 10 approved spenders. Among them were Uniswap's Permit2, the 1inch router and Bebop's settlement contract. It tracked 161 approved tokens. In all, 613 allowances were above zero.

Only the people who run it have a reason to call it. It has no deposit function and no user balances. Tokens sent to it by mistake can't be pulled back by the sender. A call to a function it lacks succeeds and does nothing.

10. The emergency brake

Any staff key can stop the bot. Only the multisig can start it again.

Pool callbacks don't check the switch. A failed zeroing is only logged. The allowance stays open. The token still drops off the bot's list (lines 416–426).

On that day, stopping it would emit 614 logs. That fits in one block (note 5).

The stop function 
function killContract() external {

11. What a reviewer would flag

Five design facts stand out. None is a claim that anything has gone wrong.

  1. The registry holds the keys. It names the transferers and the recipients. Together those two lists can send out any token or ETH. The registry's own code is not verified on Sourcify or Blockscout. So its rules, including its delay, can't be read.
  2. The Curve payment trusts the transaction's sender. It pays any listed Curve pool that asks. The pool names the coin and the amount. An operator must have signed the transaction. It checks tx.origin, not whether the bot started the swap. Curve pools join with no factory check (lines 285–287).
  3. A failed revoke drops the token anyway. The token leaves the list first. Then its allowance is zeroed. A log is the only trace of a failure (lines 416–426).
  4. Some results are ignored. Nothing checks that these worked: ETH payouts (line 489), builder tips (686), pool payments (589, 656, 665, 669, 678).
  5. A stored timelock does nothing here. A one-day delay is set at deploy. It is only read back. This contract never enforces it. See lines 128, 176, 362.

12. What we checked, and what we didn't

The verified code matches what runs on chain. Equivalence for every input is not proven. The flow figures are one day at 5-minute prices.

Checked

  • Built with its exact compiler settings, the source gives the deployed code byte for byte. Deploy arguments match too.
  • We wrote a shorter, readable copy. It has the same 63 functions, argument types and storage layout.
  • Both ran one test that calls every external function. All 331 compared results matched.
  • We ran both on a local copy of mainnet at block 26,415,084. They gave the same swap output. Both zeroed the same 613 allowances.
  • Role holders: the replay of the Safe's registry calls matched the bot's getters for every account.
  • The day: every transaction in the window was fetched with its receipt, none sampled.

Not checked

  • The metadata hash differs. The verified file is a rebuild with stand-in names (note 6).
  • Equivalence for every possible input. Tests are examples, not a proof.
  • The outside contracts: the registry, the multisig's signers, the venues and the spenders.
  • Who runs it. Explorer labels name a firm. We did not confirm them.
  • Prices finer than 5 minutes, and 4 of the day's 90 tokens that had no price. Swaps with an unpriced leg are left out of markouts.
  • Any day but this one. A different day could look different.

Method

  • Roles. We decoded every Safe execTransaction to the registry and replayed propose, execute and revoke in order. Then we called the bot's own getter for every replayed holder.
  • Flows. We pulled every ERC-20 Transfer log to or from the bot in the window, then each transaction and its receipt. A transaction is a swap if the bot both sent and received tokens. It is funding if it only received, and a payout if it only sent.
  • Markout. For each swap: the dollar value in minus the dollar value out, at 5-minute prices. The range is a bootstrap over transactions. A positive markout means the bot gained.
  • Tips and gas. The tip is the first argument of each swap function. Gas is gas used times the effective gas price. Outside senders pay their own gas, so it is not counted.
  • Inventory. getAllApprovedTokens plus balanceOf and decimals at each snapshot block, priced at that block's time.

13. How to check this yourself

A block explorer shows any contract's code, state and traffic without signing anything.

  1. Open the address on Blockscout or Etherscan.

  2. Pick the Contract tab. A check mark means the source is verified. It was compiled and matched to the code on chain.

  3. Read the Code view. Search it for the function names you see. The selectors in the appendix map them.

  4. Open Read Contract to see its current state. Reading sends nothing. Open any example transaction above to see its token transfers.

  5. Leave Write Contract alone. It asks your wallet to sign a transaction.

Words used on this page

Ethereum words

  • MEV. Profit from choosing which trades go in a block, and in what order. ethereum.org explains it.
  • Block builder. The party that packs transactions into a block. The bot reads its address from block.coinbase.
  • WETH. Wrapped ETH: ETH held as a token, so contracts can trade it like one.
  • Allowance. Permission one account gives another to pull a set amount of a token. Defined in ERC-20.
  • Markout. What a trade gained or lost against a reference price, here 5-minute prices at the trade's time. One basis point (bp) is a hundredth of a percent.

Contract words

  • Multisig. A wallet that needs several keys to sign. This one is a Safe.
  • RFQ. Request for quote: a trader asks a market maker for a signed price.
  • EIP-1271. A standard question a contract answers: did your key sign this?
  • tx.origin. The account that signed the whole transaction, not the direct caller.
  • Solver. A party that settles batches of user orders on CoW Protocol. UniswapX has fillers, which fill single orders. Both can use liquidity such as this bot's.
